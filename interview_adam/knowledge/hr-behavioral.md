## Tell me about yourself

type: question
difficulty: junior
tags: introduction, narrative, motivation, opening answer

Interviewers use this to check that your story is coherent, not to hear your CV read aloud. Answer in
ninety seconds with a shape: where you are now in one sentence, the two or three experiences that
explain the direction you have taken, and why this role is the next logical step. Choose details that
preview what you want to be asked about — the project whose trade-offs you can defend, the problem you
enjoyed. Cut childhood, unrelated jobs, and personal history the role does not need. Close on the
present and forward: "that is why I applied here" hands the conversation back. A common junior mistake
is recency without relevance, listing the most recent task instead of the most instructive one.

## What is your greatest weakness

type: question
difficulty: junior
tags: self awareness, growth, weakness, feedback

They are testing honesty and self-monitoring, not humility. A fabricated strength in disguise — "I am
too organised", "I work too hard" — reads as an unwillingness to be candid. Pick a real, specific,
non-core weakness, show its cost, and describe the concrete countermeasure you are running, with the
progress it has produced. "I interrupt when I am excited about a solution; I now write the point down
and let the other person finish, and my last project review said the discussion felt balanced" is a
complete answer. Avoid anything that undermines the job you are applying for, and avoid a weakness with
no remedy, which leaves the interviewer wondering whether you will need managing. The transferable
signal is that you can see yourself from outside.

## Describe a conflict with a teammate

type: question
difficulty: intermediate
tags: conflict, collaboration, disagreement, empathy, resolution

What is being assessed is whether you can disagree without escalating or surrendering. Show a real
technical or organisational disagreement, the reason you held your position, how you surfaced the
underlying constraint the other person was protecting — deadlines, ownership, a past incident — and
what you did to test the alternatives rather than argue. Name the concrete step: a short spike, a
written comparison, asking a third person for a tie-break. State the outcome honestly, including when
the other option won and what you learned from it. Never end the story with "I was right", and never
describe a person as the problem; blame language is the fastest way to fail this question, because the
interviewer assumes you will do the same to them.

## Tell me about a failure

type: question
difficulty: intermediate
tags: failure, ownership, learning, accountability, resilience

Take one failure with a real cost and own it in the first sentence. Explain the decision you made, what
you believed at the time, and the exact point where your assumption was wrong. Then separate the two
things interviewers want: the fix under pressure, and the structural change that prevents a repeat — a
test you now write, a review you now request, a threshold you now monitor. Quantify the damage if you
can; vagueness reads as evasion. Finish with what you do differently and one piece of evidence that the
change stuck. Avoid the three classic traps: blaming a manager or a teammate, choosing a failure that
was really someone else's, and picking a trivial stumble that shows you have never risked anything.

## A time you had to meet a tight deadline

type: question
difficulty: junior
tags: prioritisation, deadline, scope, communication, pressure

The answer they want is about judgement, not about how much you suffered. Describe the constraint, how
you decided what to cut — scope, polish, or a nice-to-have — and how you communicated the trade-off to
the person who could approve it. Show the mechanism: a minimum shippable version, a checklist, an
explicit "here is what will not make it" note sent early rather than a silence broken at the deadline.
Mention what you protected: correctness, security, the rollback path. Then close the loop: what you
learned about estimating, and the standing change you made afterwards, such as timeboxing a task you
had sized by optimism. "I worked all night" alone is a warning sign, not an achievement — it says the
plan failed and the person is one crisis away from repeating it.

## Handling critical feedback you did not agree with

type: question
difficulty: intermediate
tags: feedback, coachability, ego, dialogue, improvement

Coachability is tested directly here. A good answer asks a clarifying question before defending, gets
a concrete example, and separates the content of the feedback from the delivery. Then show both
outcomes you are capable of: if the criticism was fair, the specific habit you changed and how you
verified it; if it was mistaken, how you presented the missing context without turning it into a
dispute, and what you agreed on. Avoid the two failure modes — accepting everything unquestioningly,
which suggests you will implement whatever is asked, and rebutting everything, which suggests you are
expensive to manage. Ending with "I asked for a follow-up review in a month" signals you treat
feedback as a loop rather than an event.

## Why do you want to work here

type: question
difficulty: junior
tags: motivation, research, company, preparation, fit

Generic enthusiasm fails this question; evidence passes it. Show you looked at what the company actually
does — a product you used, an engineering blog post, an open-source release, a public position on
technology or hiring — and connect it to something specific in your own track record. Then name what
you want that only this context provides: a domain you want depth in, a scale of problem, a team whose
review culture you have seen described. Honest ambition is fine; flattery is not. Do not build the
answer on compensation, prestige, or "a great opportunity to learn" alone, which reads as wanting
whatever is offered. Two sentences of research plus one sentence of self-placement beats a paragraph
of praise.

## Where do you see yourself in five years

type: question
difficulty: junior
tags: career goals, ambition, planning, growth

They want direction and realism, not a title. Sketch a trajectory in terms of responsibility and depth:
owning a service end to end, becoming the person teammates ask about a domain, mentoring someone junior,
or moving toward architecture or toward a specialised track — pick one and be honest that you might
change your mind. Tie it to what the role plausibly offers so the answer is not a fantasy; a candidate
aiming at a staff architect role inside a two-person team has not thought about the environment. Avoid
declaring you want the interviewer's job, and avoid "I do not know" with nothing after it. A short
sentence about what stays constant — building things that get used, learning from review — is more
convincing than a five-year plan nobody believes.

## Working remotely and communicating asynchronously

type: question
difficulty: intermediate
tags: remote work, communication, async, documentation, autonomy

Remote work rewards people who make their state visible without being asked. Talk about the practice:
a written summary of a decision with the options considered, a daily note where the work can be read,
issues updated before the end of the day, an explicit request for review with links, and a bias toward
docs over meetings. Mention how you handle the failure modes: a blocker raised early rather than
nursed, time zones respected by recording context instead of demanding immediacy, and quiet periods
used for depth work rather than interpreted as absence. Give a concrete instance where writing things
down prevented a misunderstanding. Do not frame it as self-discipline in the abstract; interviewers
listen for whether you will be invisible for three days and then produce a surprise.

## Leading without formal authority

type: question
difficulty: senior
tags: leadership, influence, initiative, ownership, mentoring

Seniority shows up as influence exercised from inside the team. Tell the story of a gap you noticed and
chose to close without being assigned: a missing test harness, an undocumented deployment, an unclear
requirement that was stalling everyone. Cover the persuasion, not just the work — how you got the
sceptical colleague on board, how you limited the risk of the change, how you handed ownership back so
the improvement did not depend on you. Include mentoring: reviewing someone's pull request so they find
the bug themselves, running a short session, writing the guide you wished existed. A strong answer
acknowledges a cost — time taken from your own delivery, or a decision you made that the team later
revised — because influence without a single success is a story about luck.

## How do you handle not knowing an answer

type: question
difficulty: junior
tags: honesty, reasoning, uncertainty, problem solving, integrity

Fabricating an answer is the failure this question is built to catch. The credible move is to say what
you know, what you would guess, and what you would check — then reason out loud from principles you
do hold. "I have not used that library; I would expect it behaves like X, and I would confirm it with a
fifteen-minute spike before committing" shows judgement and integrity at once. For a technical
discussion, being able to narrow the problem is worth more than a memorised flag: ask what breaks,
where the evidence would be, which layer is failing. Never bluff about production experience — a
follow-up question exposes it and puts every other answer in doubt. In a take-home or pairing session,
narrate the search: reading the error, isolating, reverting, and stating the moment you would escalate.

## Questions for us at the end

type: guideline
difficulty: junior
tags: candidate questions, preparation, team, curiosity

Asking nothing signals no curiosity; asking only about holidays signals no interest in the work.
Prepare three or four that you genuinely want answered and that also reveal fit. About the team: how
reviews work, who mentors a new joiner in the first month, what the last production incident taught
them. About the craft: how a feature goes from idea to deployed, what the test and deployment story
looks like, what technical debt they would fix first. About expectations: what would make them say after
six months that this hire was right. About the interviewer's own experience, which is where the honest
answers live. Close on process: next steps and timeline. Avoid anything easily answered by the
landing page, and do not open with compensation on a first interview — that belongs with the recruiter.

## Negotiating compensation professionally

type: guideline
difficulty: senior
tags: negotiation, salary, market value, offer, total compensation

Negotiate on market data and scope, never on personal need. Establish a range before you are asked:
levels from published bands and comparable roles, adjusted for the city, the seniority and the whole
package — bonus, equity, pension, learning budget, remote policy, notice period. When an offer lands,
be enthusiastic about the role and specific about the number: a single figure you would sign at,
reasoned, then silence. Ask about the levers that are not salary if the band is fixed — a signing bonus,
an earlier review date, a title, a training budget, extra leave. Never bluff with a competing offer you
do not hold, and do not burn the relationship over a small difference; the same people may be your
managers. Get every commitment in writing in the contract, because the verbal concession does not exist.
